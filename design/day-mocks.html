The position

A flash is an event: it starts at a knowable moment, it may end, it has an intensity. Tinnitus is not an event. It was there when she woke and it is still there now. Asking "when did your brain fog start?" produces a made-up answer, and this app does not keep made-up answers.

So the second record is a day check-in: one row per local date, holding a severity for each symptom she chose to report, plus a note. It is not a timeline entry with a fuzzy time — it is a state of a day, and it is modelled as one. An untouched symptom is stored as nothing at all, which is different from None, which she tapped deliberately. "I didn't check in" and "I checked and it was fine" are not the same fact.

The record is keyed on the date, so writing it is an upsert. That falls out beautifully offline: replaying the same queued check-in twice cannot create a duplicate, so the outbox needs no client-id trick and last-tap-wins is always correct.

What this gives up. Intra-day resolution: there is no way to say the tinnitus stopped at four o'clock, and no way to record two different severities for the same symptom on the same day — the value is the worst of the day. Both are deliberate. A woman who is asked to timestamp her joint pain will stop answering by Wednesday, and the answers she does give will be guesses. One honest number per day is worth more than twelve invented ones.

Unset is not None

Fatigue and Low mood have nothing selected, and they are stored as nothing — no row, no zero, no default. Joint pain says None, which she tapped, and that is a real observation worth keeping. The same refusal to invent that leaves a slept-through flash with no end time.

Every tap upserts immediately, so there is no submit button to forget. Yesterday and the day before are reachable with the chevron — poor sleep is remembered in the morning, not at 4am.

Four steps, not ten. A ten-point scale invites false precision on something she is estimating anyway, and it makes the row too small to hit.

Log Day History Export

The day is the weather, flashes are the events

A day-level state has no time, so it cannot be interleaved with timed rows without pretending it has one. Instead it becomes a band across the head of the day it belongs to: the conditions, and then what happened inside them. Nothing gets a fake timestamp and the list stays scannable.

Friday has a check-in and no flashes and is still a group, because "quiet day, still ringing" is a finding. Thursday says plainly that nobody checked in, rather than showing an empty band that could be read as "nothing wrong". Wednesday is the third case, and it is a different fact from both: she did check in and everything came back None.

Only mild and above get a chip. Saturday's joint pain was answered None and is not shown — a band of nothings buries the one line that matters, and history is for scanning. The zeroes are not thrown away: they stay in the record, in the CSV, and in Avg severity below, which is where "was the tinnitus ever quiet?" gets answered.

The statistics split rather than merge. There is no honest single number spanning an eleven-minute episode and a day of tinnitus, so the strip becomes two labelled strips. Avg severity is the mean of the severities she actually gave — unreported symptoms are absent from it, not counted as zero, though a tapped None does count. Most reported ignores None entirely: answering "no joint pain" every morning is diligence, not suffering.

Two spreadsheets, because the two records don't share columns — a day has no start time and a flash has no severity per symptom. Forcing them into one table would leave half of every row blank. The JSON holds both, and each sketch as the strokes you drew.

From Siri

Build a shortcut named Log tinnitus and "Hey Siri, log tinnitus" asks mild, moderate or severe, then writes it to today's check-in. One shortcut per symptom, since Siri calls them by name — the setup is written up here. Saying it twice just corrects the answer: a check-in is keyed on the date, so it can never be logged twice.

Shape of the export

The flash CSV keeps its exact columns, so a spreadsheet already sitting on a GP's desk does not break. The day CSV is one row per date with a column per symptom, which is the shape a clinician reads without being taught it.

The JSON gains a days array and a second vocabulary block, and bumps to version 2. It stays self-describing: a file opened in five years still explains what brain_fog meant and what the four severities were.

Two records, one principle. A flash is something that happened; a day check-in is how a day was. Neither is allowed to invent the thing it does not know — a flash slept through has no end time, and a symptom she never mentioned has no severity.
